It remembers who walked in.

A local computer-vision monitor. Point it at a webcam or a CCTV stream and it tracks everyone in frame, gives each person a number that survives them leaving the room, and tells you only about the moments worth knowing.

What it does

Five things, all on your machine

Identity

A number that sticks

Faces become 512 numbers. Walk out, walk back in tomorrow, and you are still #1. No enrolment step and no training — it is a nearest-neighbour lookup in a file you own.

Tracking

Several people at once

Each person keeps their id across frames, so labels never swap when the detector reorders its list. Their clothes, action and history follow them, not their position in an array.

Description

Clothing and action

Colour is sampled straight from pixels. Garment and hat types come from CLIP, which means the vocabulary is a list of sentences you can edit rather than a model you have to retrain.

Alert

Covered faces

A mask, balaclava, helmet or scarf turns the box red and pulses it. It fails open on purpose: an unsure reading raises nothing, because an alarm that cries wolf gets ignored.

Notify

Only interesting events

Discord gets a message when someone first appears, when they change what they are doing, and when a face gets covered. Standing still produces silence.

Local

Nothing leaves

Face vectors, snapshots and config all stay on disk. The only thing that goes out is the webhook message you configured yourself.

The finding

The engine had to be replaced

The whole thing started on MediaPipe, and multi-person looked like a one-line change: set num_poses to four. It isn't. MediaPipe returns one person per frame in practice, and it took a while to accept that rather than assume I had wired it wrong.

Test on a two-person frameResult

Confidence dropped to 0.5 / 0.3 / 0.11 person, every time

So not confidence, not model size, not the video-tracking path. It is single-subject by nature, which makes it the wrong tool for a doorway. YOLOv8-pose finds both people at 0.93 and 0.92 confidence in 16 ms, and became the default. MediaPipe stayed selectable — its 33 landmarks beat YOLO's 17 when you only care about one person.

Its keypoints get written into MediaPipe's 33-slot layout, so every feature downstream was written once and works with either backend.

Discord

A log that stays readable

The first version posted on a timer and it was useless within a minute — a person sitting at a desk generated a message every ninety seconds saying nothing had changed. Posting is now driven by events, and an action has to hold steady for most of a second before it counts as one. A flicker across the walk/stand threshold is not news.

Each carries a snapshot. Everything else the system knows — match score, garment types, hat, action history — stays in the app, where you can look at it if you want it.

The middle row is the interesting one. Adding CLIP cost seven frames a second and almost no CPU, because it runs on the GPU and the video loop was simply standing there waiting for it. Moving it onto a worker thread bought the frame rate back for about five hundredths of a core.

Straight answers

What it does not do

It will not name a stranger

Recognition is a lookup against faces it has already seen. A new face gets the next free number, nothing more.

It gives up on small faces

Under 60 pixels wide the embedding stops meaning anything, so it declines instead of guessing. That is most of a room-wide CCTV shot.

It cannot see through people

Heavy occlusion still splits one person into two tracks. Boxes overlapping is fine; a body genuinely cut in half is not.

It does not guess age or gender

Left out deliberately. Those classifiers are unreliable enough that a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer.

Devblog · Thu 13 Aug 2026

A webcam toy that turned into a monitor

It started as the smallest possible thing: open the webcam, draw a skeleton, show the frame rate. One file, about a hundred lines. It ended the same day as a desktop app that watches a CCTV stream, recognises faces and files reports to Discord. Almost none of that was planned.

The first hour was not computer vision

The skeleton worked on the first try. What did not work was the camera. OpenCV opened index 0 and got the OBS virtual camera, which sits there permanently as a system extension. Index 1 gave a black frame at a healthy thirty frames a second — a valid stream carrying nothing.

The answer was that an iPhone had attached as a Continuity Camera, inserted itself into the device list, and pushed every real camera down a slot. Same index, different hardware, depending on whether a phone happens to be nearby. I had confidently mapped the indices from system_profiler and been wrong, because that is not the order OpenCV uses.

Colour was harder than pose

Reading what someone is wearing sounds like the easy half. Sample the torso, sample the legs, name the colour. Three bugs came out of that, and all three produced confident, plausible, wrong answers:

Measuring from the midpoint of the hips down to a knee looks correct until somebody stands with their legs apart, at which point the line crosses open floor and you report the colour of the carpet. Fixing that by measuring each leg from its own hip broke it differently — sampling halfway down a thigh assumes long trousers, and on shorts you are reading skin. And judging "black" by darkness alone labels navy denim black, because real jeans come out at HSV(111, 255, 51): dark, but fully saturated, and therefore blue.

None of these show up unless you check against something you already know the answer to. All of them look fine in a live preview.

The model that would not count past one

Multi-person was where the build changed shape. The full account is above; the short version is that I spent longer than I would like proving a library could not do something, rather than assuming I had misused it. The useful habit was testing the negative case: crop each person out alone, confirm both are individually detectable, then show the whole frame still returns one. That is the difference between "my code is broken" and "this tool does not do this".

Prompts are code, and they are position-dependent

Garment and hat types come from CLIP, which scores an image against sentences. Writing "a photo of a person wearing a baseball cap" and pointing it at a head crop gave a confident headphones at 0.78 on a man with messy hair. Rewriting it as "a close-up of a head wearing a baseball cap" took the same crop to 1.00.

The same change made the torso worse. A torso crop includes shoulders and arms, so it really does look like a photo of a person, and person-centric wording wins there. The prompt has to match what is actually inside the rectangle, and the only way to know is to try both and read the numbers.

Faces were the least difficult part

Identity felt like the ambitious feature and turned out to be the tamest. There is no training: a pretrained network turns a face into 512 numbers, and recognition is a nearest-neighbour lookup. Two photos of me scored 0.886. Me against a different person scored 0.00. With a threshold at 0.40 sitting in that gap, it is not a close call.

The engineering that mattered was refusing to answer. Under sixty pixels of face the vector still comes back looking confident while meaning very little, which is exactly how a recogniser starts inventing people. A 40-pixel face gets declined instead.

The webhook taught me what an event is

The first Discord integration posted every person every ninety seconds with a full field grid: clothing, action, hat, match score, source. It was unreadable inside two minutes, and the fix was two separate realisations. Fewer fields — an id and a picture is enough, and everything else belongs in the app. And no timer at all: post when something happens, where "something" has to hold steady for most of a second, because the action classifier oscillates around its own thresholds and a flicker is not an event.

What I would keep

Every real bug in this build was found by checking against something whose answer I already knew — a photo of a man in a black mask, a pair of blue jeans, two shots of my own face. The live preview looked correct the entire time. It is very easy to build a system that is confidently wrong and watch it run at thirty frames a second, feeling productive.
